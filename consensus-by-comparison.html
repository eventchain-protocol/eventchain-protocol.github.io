3  Consensus by Comparison

Modified

October 14, 2026

The members of a ledger agree on the history before each append, by a round of votes among everyone present. The holders of an EventChain history agree after the append, each by comparing its copy with one line the Hub has signed and published (a heartbeat), and nobody has to be present. Both routes end in the same state: every party holds one history, and each knows that any other party checking against the same heartbeat finds the same history through the entry it covers. Because each holder applies the same rules to an identical copy, each reaches the same verdict on whether an entry follows them; only the payload check depends on what each holder may read (Section 4.7). That state is what consensus guarantees, and this paper uses the word for the guarantee. The two routes differ in when the agreement happens and who has to be present. The costs and limits of each route follow from that difference.

In the same way, a history with two or more holders is a distributed ledger in which the parties hold the copies and one Hub sets the order: many producers write, one Hub orders each topic, holders read, and no holder’s check depends on another holder.1 This chapter says “a ledger” for the kind that votes, and a history with one holder is a signed log.

3.1 What You Get

EventChain gives six of the eight guarantees people seek from a ledger without a round of votes, each under an assumption that Table 3.1 names, and leaves the other two to contract and to other layers.

The history is a text file that anyone can check with SHA-256 and ECDSA-P256, so the evidence outlives the platform that produced it.2 Every holder verifies every entry of its history, including entries whose payloads access policy keeps from it (Section 4.7). A gap in the chain or a copy shorter than the latest heartbeat shows that entries are missing, so a holder notices when a history leaves out inconvenient entries.3

3.2 What It Costs

Because nobody has to be present at the append, an append is one write, and the work of recording grows with the number of events and with nothing else. Picture an electricity supplier with 10,000 meters, each signing a reading every fifteen minutes (worked example): 960,000 entries a day, about eleven a second. Whether the supplier can keep that history depends on whether the work grows with the number of events or with the number of parties who hold the history. On a ledger it grows with both.

In 2018, Hyperledger Fabric’s authors at IBM Research reported more than 3,500 transactions a second for a payment workload with five peers in one data centre, falling to between 1,400 and 2,000 with 100 peers across five data centres, every peer holding the whole ledger (Androulaki et al. 2018). The whole Ethereum network processed 3.6 million transactions on 28 April 2026, its busiest day up to October 2026 (Etherscan 2026); this one supplier’s readings would equal a quarter of that day. A consortium ledger recovers its costs from its users: in 2021, TradeLens listed its service from $8 per container (TradeLens 2021).

Suppose another logistics company joins a shipment process. Figure 3.1 follows the carrier’s signed acceptance: the company joins the consortium ledger as a validating member, or receives the EventChain history as a holder.

A ledger can also serve read-only users without adding validators. Such a user trusts the validators for what an EventChain holder checks itself from its copy. Adding an EventChain holder adds one copy of the file and that holder’s own checks; existing holders do not repeat verification for it. The application’s business rules still apply: if dispatch requires the new company’s acceptance, the dispatch system waits for it (Section 4.7).

The Hub checks each submitted line as a verifier does, for well-formedness, predecessor hash and signature, before it appends (Section 4.4). A benchmark of the reference Hub on 14 October 2026, on one thread per topic, committed more than 11,000 entries a second with all three checks on every line, and 29,000 with the head comparison alone, from one run (EventChain 2026a); the checks cost the Hub thread 65 microseconds a line (Section 4.5). No fee attaches to an event. Each holder receives one line of about 335 bytes per event and verifies its copy on its own processor at its own pace. A holder that verified yesterday’s copy checks only today’s new entries. These measurements describe different workloads and environments: observed Ethereum traffic is not network capacity, and the figures are not a comparative throughput benchmark.

Batching spreads the trip to the Hub across many events. A benchmark of the reference producer on 13 October 2026 measured it against a compare-and-append Hub model (EventChain 2026c). Each producer signed a sequence of entries, each over the one before, and submitted them together. The Hub model accepted a batch when its first entry followed the current head, and the reference Hub checks every line before writing. That first check binds the batch to the whole history. The benchmark then checked format and signatures on every output file, and all 160 passed. Table 3.2 shows the committed entries per second with a 20-millisecond in-flight delay; the measured delay was about 21 milliseconds.

Each event keeps its own signature and its own line in the file. Larger batches spread the delay across more entries, so throughput approaches one producer’s signing rate. Producers that race for the same head spend more CPU: under saturating load, ten producers make about ten signatures per committed entry, because each loser signs its batch again against the new head. Larger batches also make producers wait longer. With ten producers and batches of 3,000 at this delay, median latency from the first signature to acceptance was 1.02 seconds, and the 99th percentile was 3.29 seconds; an event also waits for its batch to fill. Some producers committed nothing during the four-second windows, so a process that needs independent producers to progress gives them topics of their own (Section 4.5).

Some work costs the same with a ledger or with EventChain: a deployment still pays to integrate existing systems and to enrol signers. Ledger consortia carried one more item: those that closed in 2022 and 2023 named reaching critical mass as what consumed the money (Section 2.3.1). EventChain has no such item, because one organisation can start and the others take the evidence when they need it.

3.3 What Remains Trusted

Beyond the file and a verifier, a holder depends only on what the last column of Table 3.1 names, chiefly the enrolment evidence behind each key, the heartbeat hosts the foundation lists for its topic and the Hub’s availability to accept new entries. The Hub cannot rewrite a signed entry. It can withhold entries from a holder or show different holders different histories; Figure 4.2 shows how a holder checks against the latest heartbeat. Two conflicting heartbeats under the Hub’s key attribute the fork to the Hub. The field calls these properties fork consistency and accountability (Section 4.4; adversarial Hub analysis). Deciding what to do with the evidence stays with the application. EventChain does not prevent a false entry at the moment of creation; it makes later denial and editing unattractive, because each entry names a signing key and binds its statement to the preceding history.4 Table 4 gives the boundary capability by capability.

3.4 Where It Breaks

A valid signature proves that a key signed these bytes. It cannot reveal an honest mistake, coercion, collusion or a signer who signs without reading. Process controls and audit remain necessary, and when something goes wrong they now start from a signed history and the identity evidence behind it (failure-mode tables).

3.5 When It Fits

EventChain fits where each party to a process answers for what it signs, and where at least one of them needs evidence that survives beyond any single platform. Because each party answers for what it signs, detection after the fact suffices. Because the evidence travels as a file, nobody has to join a network before the first entry is useful. Where strangers settle payments with one another, a ledger’s round of votes remains the right tool. Where one organisation controls the whole process and nobody outside needs to verify, an ordinary audit trail does. Table 9 sets each approach beside the others.
